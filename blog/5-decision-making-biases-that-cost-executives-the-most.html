The 5 Decision-Making Biases That Cost Executives the Most

These five cognitive biases show up in executive decision-making more consistently than any others — and are most likely to be invisible without a systematic decision record.

April 2026 · 9 min read

The research on cognitive bias is extensive. There are dozens of named biases, hundreds of studies, and an entire sub-industry of books and workshops dedicated to identifying and correcting them. Most of it doesn't work — not because the biases aren't real, but because awareness alone is not enough to change behaviour.

What actually works is a feedback loop. When you can see, in your own decision history, that a specific bias is consistently showing up — in your stated confidence levels, your expected outcomes, your review notes — you can act on it specifically. Abstract knowledge becomes concrete data.

These are the five biases that show up most consistently in executive decision records, and that cause the most damage when they go undetected.

1. Overconfidence bias

What it is: The tendency to overestimate the accuracy of your judgements and the likelihood that your preferred outcome will occur. Overconfidence is the most well-documented bias in professional decision-making across virtually every domain studied.

How it shows up: High confidence scores that don't correspond to high success rates. Decisions made at 90% confidence that succeed only 60–65% of the time. Plans that assume the optimistic case without explicitly modelling alternatives. Timelines and budgets that have no contingency.

How to detect it: Track your confidence calibration over time. If your high-confidence band systematically underperforms your stated confidence level, overconfidence is present. It's nearly impossible to detect reliably without the data — the subjective experience of being overconfident feels identical to the experience of being appropriately confident.

2. Sunk cost bias

What it is: The tendency to continue investing in a course of action because of past investments, even when the expected future return no longer justifies it. Sunk costs — money, time, reputational investment — are irreversible and should be irrelevant to forward-looking decisions. They rarely are.

How it shows up: Continuing to fund or resource a project or initiative well past the point where the evidence supports it. Holding positions in underperforming assets because of the original purchase price. Persisting with a hire who isn't working because of the effort spent recruiting them. Escalating commitment to a strategy that has consistently failed to deliver as originally modelled.

How to detect it: When reviewing a decision to continue or exit something, look at your decision journal entry for when the original commitment was made. Ask explicitly: if this were a new decision — if the prior investment had never happened — would I make it now? If the answer is no, you may be in sunk cost territory. Logging continuation decisions as new decisions (not just continuations) makes this visible.

3. Availability bias

What it is: The tendency to overweight information that is easily recalled — recent events, vivid examples, personally experienced outcomes — relative to statistically representative evidence. What comes to mind easily feels more probable than it actually is.

How it shows up: After a market crash, overweighting crash risk in subsequent decisions. After a successful hire, overconfidence in a similar hire. After a high-profile competitor failure, excessive caution in a related area. Overfitting strategy to the last cycle. Treating the most memorable data point as the most representative one.

How to detect it: In decision notes, look for reasoning that relies heavily on a single recent example or personal experience as the primary evidence base. Ask: what is the base rate for this type of decision across a broader reference class? Availability bias tends to produce expected outcomes that mirror recent experience rather than long-run averages. Reviewing your decision history across a multi-year window helps correct for the recency effect.

4. Confirmation bias

What it is: The tendency to seek, interpret, and recall information in a way that confirms your pre-existing beliefs or hypotheses. Once a view is formed, the mind tends to filter evidence in its favour. Disconfirming evidence is underweighted, discounted, or simply not sought.

How it shows up: Due diligence processes that produce findings consistent with a pre-formed thesis. Investment cases where risks are consistently described as "manageable" regardless of their actual severity. Strategy reviews that reliably conclude in favour of the strategy being reviewed. A pattern of decision rationale notes that focus heavily on the supporting evidence and are thin on genuine challenges to the thesis.

How to detect it: Review the rationale notes from past decisions and examine the ratio of confirming to disconfirming evidence considered. Well-calibrated decisions usually engage seriously with the strongest case against the chosen course of action. If your notes consistently lack this, confirmation bias may be shaping what gets written down. Structured pre-mortems and designated devil's advocates are the most effective process-level countermeasures.

5. Action bias

What it is: The preference for action over inaction, even when the evidence for acting is no stronger than the evidence for waiting. Action bias is driven partly by the social optics of leadership — decisiveness is valued, patience can be misread as hesitancy — and partly by the asymmetric regret that people feel about errors of omission versus errors of commission.

How it shows up: Hiring when the right candidate hasn't been found rather than holding the role open longer. Making strategic moves under competitive pressure before the analysis is complete. Restructuring or reorganising as a response to underperformance when the underlying issue is not structural. "Doing something" in response to a crisis when the optimal response is to monitor and wait.

How to detect it: In your decision records, tag decisions that were made under time pressure or competitive pressure. Review what the rationale was for acting at that specific moment rather than later. If the rationale is primarily situational pressure rather than information readiness, action bias may have been the driver. Over a large enough decision history, you will be able to see whether time-pressured decisions have lower success rates than equivalent decisions made without that pressure.

Why awareness alone isn't enough

Every executive has heard of most of these biases. Most can define them. Very few have any data on which ones affect their own decision-making, in which specific contexts, and with what frequency. The gap between knowing about a bias and correcting for it in your own behaviour is bridged only by a feedback loop that makes the bias visible in your own record.

You cannot correct a bias you cannot see. And you cannot see it in your own decision history without a decision record.

A decision journal — maintained consistently, with confidence scores recorded at decision time — makes each of these biases detectable. Overconfidence shows in the calibration curve. Sunk cost shows in continuation decisions that don't survive the "new decision" test. Availability shows in expected outcomes that cluster around recent experience. Confirmation shows in thin rationale notes. Action bias shows in the success rate of decisions made under pressure versus without it.
