I tracked every significant decision I made for 90 days. Here's what I found.
Post Body
About eighteen months ago I made a bad hiring decision. Not catastrophically bad — the person wasn't dishonest or incompetent — but wrong for the role in ways I could have seen if I'd looked harder. They left after six months. We were both quietly relieved. What bothered me wasn't the hire itself. It was that I'd been genuinely confident about it. I remember telling my co-founder something like "I'm pretty sure this is the right person." And afterwards, doing the post-mortem in my head, I found I couldn't reliably reconstruct how confident I'd actually been. Had I had doubts? I thought I had — but maybe I was just revising history to make the outcome feel more predictable. That bothered me enough to start keeping a log. For 90 days I wrote down every significant decision I made: what I was deciding, what I expected to happen, and a confidence score from 50% (coin flip) to 95% (near-certain). I set reminders to check back at 30 and 90 days and record what actually happened. Here's what the data showed. **People decisions: I was systematically overconfident.** Every significant hiring or partnership decision I logged, I'd assigned 75–80% confidence. The actual success rate at 90 days was closer to 55%. That's not noise — it's a consistent gap. I was treating genuinely uncertain outcomes as near-probable while telling myself I was being careful and thorough. **Operational decisions: I was reasonably well calibrated.** Vendor choices, process changes, tooling — my 70% confidence calls were succeeding around 70% of the time. My 85% calls were landing somewhere in the 80–85% range. I was actually quite good at this category. I had no idea, because I'd never looked. **Strategic decisions: all over the place.** Longer-horizon calls about direction, priorities, where to focus — my confidence scores bore almost no relationship to outcomes. I was essentially guessing with the self-assurance of someone who wasn't. But none of that was the most valuable thing I found in those 90 days. The most valuable moment was reading what I'd written before I knew how things turned out. There's one decision from the start of that period where I wrote, in my notes, something like: *"I have a nagging feeling I'm not asking the right questions about X, but I'm going ahead anyway."* I'd noted my confidence as 80%. The decision failed. Reading that note back months later, I felt something I can only describe as being confronted by an earlier, more honest version of myself. I had known something was off. I'd written it down and then proceeded as if I hadn't. If I'd had that note surface as a prompt at 30 days — when there might still have been something to do about it — the outcome could have been different. I've kept the log going since then. It's now over a year of data and the patterns have only gotten clearer. Does anyone else do something like this? I'm curious what formats or approaches others use, whether paper, spreadsheet, or something else. (The tool I built to solve this: reflect-os.com.)

I built a tool for a problem I had. Here's what 8 months of dogfooding taught me.
Post Body
Eight months ago I launched a product I'd built to solve a problem I'd had for years — tracking decisions and reviewing them systematically. I've been using it as my primary tool since before it was publicly live, which means I have a fairly clear picture of what actually works and what I got wrong. Here are the four things that genuinely surprised me. **1. People are overconfident in predictable, category-specific ways — not randomly.** Before building this I assumed the main finding would be something generic like "you're more overconfident than you think." What the data actually shows is that overconfidence is highly domain-specific. You might be well-calibrated on financial decisions and badly calibrated on people decisions. Or great at operational calls and all over the place on strategic ones. This is more useful than generic overconfidence, because it tells you exactly where to apply more scrutiny — not everywhere indiscriminately, which is exhausting and often counterproductive. **2. The original log is more valuable than the review.** When I designed the product I thought the review — comparing what you expected to what actually happened — would be the most valuable moment. It's not. The most valuable thing is reading what you wrote at decision time, before you knew the outcome. People write differently when the outcome isn't known. They admit doubts. They flag concerns. They note the things they're not quite sure about. That version of your thinking is more honest than anything you'd reconstruct after the fact. It's also the version you most need to be able to interrogate. **3. Nobody reviews decisions without a system actively forcing them to.** I built automated review reminders in early and thought they were a nice-to-have. They're not. Every time I've spoken to a user who drifted away from the product, the pattern is the same: they logged some decisions, nothing prompted them to go back, and they gradually stopped logging too because it felt like data going nowhere. The review is what makes the log worthwhile. Without forced prompts it just becomes a journal nobody reads, including its owner. **4. Calibration data becomes useful much faster than I expected.** I assumed you'd need a year or more of records before any real patterns emerged. In practice, 15–20 logged decisions with honest confidence scores starts to show you something real about your calibration. By 50 decisions across three or four months, most people can see clear domain-level patterns. That compressed the value timeline significantly, which changed how I thought about acquisition — you don't have to promise people a 12-month payoff to get them engaged. --- What I'm still working out: how to make people take calibration data seriously once they've seen it. The first instinct is almost always to explain why the numbers are misleading in your specific case. Breaking through that defensiveness is more of a product and UX challenge than a data one. If anyone's built something where users have had to confront uncomfortable data about themselves, I'd be curious how you handled it. The tool is called Reflect OS. Live at reflect-os.com if anyone's curious.

Quora Answers
Target Question Type
Decision journals / decision-making tools / how to keep a decision journal
Answer Body
Most decision journals fail, and they almost always fail for the same reason: people record what happened instead of what they expected. You write up the outcome. Maybe a brief note on how it went. File it away. Six months later you have a chronological log of events — useful as a diary, nearly useless for improving your decision-making. The fundamental problem is that the learning only happens when you can compare an honest prior expectation against an actual outcome. If the expectation is recorded after the fact, it's contaminated by the result. This is the hindsight bias problem. Research going back to the 1970s shows clearly that people cannot accurately recall their pre-outcome beliefs once the outcome is known. Memory adjusts automatically and systematically toward what happened. The version of your confidence that you remember after a good outcome is higher than it was. After a bad outcome, lower. You cannot correct for this retrospectively — it's not a discipline problem, it's a memory architecture problem. **What to capture — and when** A decision record that actually closes the feedback loop needs four things, all captured before the outcome is known: *The decision itself* — specific enough to be evaluated later. Not "we decided to expand" but "we decided to open a second location in Bristol, targeting Q4 launch, with a £200k budget." Vague decisions cannot be honestly evaluated. *The expected outcome* — concrete and falsifiable. What do you expect to happen, and over what time horizon? "I expect this to go well" is not an expected outcome. "I expect the new product line to contribute at least 15% of revenue within 12 months" is. *Your confidence level* — a number from 50% (coin flip) to 95% (near-certain). This is the field most people resist, because a number feels more accountable than a sentiment. That accountability is exactly the point. *A review date* — when will you know whether the expected outcome happened? Set it explicitly. Without a committed date, the record stays open indefinitely and the feedback loop never closes. **What happens at review** The review is where the learning actually happens — but only because the capture happened first. You return to the record, read what you actually believed before you knew the outcome, and record what occurred. The comparison between the two is the signal. The most useful reviews surface two things: decisions where you were right but for reasons other than the ones you cited (good outcome, flawed reasoning — arguably more dangerous than a bad outcome from good reasoning, because it reinforces the wrong lesson), and decisions where you noted doubt or uncertainty at the time but proceeded as if you hadn't. **The calibration insight** Maintain this discipline across enough decisions — thirty or forty is enough to start seeing patterns — and something genuinely valuable emerges: your calibration curve. Calibration, in this context, means the relationship between your stated confidence and your actual accuracy rate. If you're well-calibrated, your 70% confidence decisions should succeed about 70% of the time. Your 85% calls should land around 85%. If they don't — and for most experienced professionals, they don't, especially at the high end — that's not random noise. It's a systematic signature of where your reasoning process is generating more certainty than the evidence warrants. This is the output that makes everything else worthwhile. Not the individual reviews, valuable as they are, but the pattern that emerges across your full decision history. The tool I built specifically for this workflow is called Reflect OS (reflect-os.com). The capture form takes under 60 seconds, review prompts are automated, and the calibration dashboard builds itself from your decision history without any manual analysis. But the core practice — capture before outcome, structured review, honest confidence scoring — is achievable with a spreadsheet if you're disciplined about it. The discipline is the hard part.

Target Question Type
What is confidence calibration / how do you improve confidence calibration
Answer Body
Confidence calibration is the alignment between how certain you feel about a belief and how accurate you actually are when you hold that level of certainty. A well-calibrated person who says "I'm 80% confident in this" is right about 80% of the time when they say that. Not on this specific claim — probability statements don't work that way — but across the population of claims they make at that confidence level. The 80% is a prediction about their own accuracy rate, not just an expression of feeling. Most people are not well-calibrated, and the dominant error is overconfidence. This has been documented across medicine, law, finance, engineering, and virtually every other professional domain studied. The pattern is consistent: experts are systematically more confident than accurate, particularly on the high-stakes infrequent decisions that define careers, as opposed to the routine decisions they make dozens of times a day. The counterintuitive finding — and this matters — is that expertise often makes calibration worse rather than better. Experience accumulates narrative and reinforces existing mental models without necessarily testing them. An investment partner who has made twenty successful sector bets doesn't just become more accurate on the next one; they often become more certain, even when the underlying conditions have shifted. Confidence and accuracy decouple in exactly the domains where you'd most want them aligned. **How to measure your calibration** You need a record of stated confidence levels at decision time — before outcomes are known — paired with eventual outcomes. Retrospective confidence is nearly worthless for this purpose because memory adjusts toward outcomes automatically. Once you have that record across enough decisions (thirty to fifty is a useful starting point), you can see your calibration curve: how your actual success rate compares to your stated confidence, across confidence bands. Most people, when they first see this data, find that their highest-confidence decisions perform significantly worse than the confidence level implies. That's the overconfidence signature. **Why it doesn't improve without a record** The reason calibration is so difficult to improve informally is that the feedback loop doesn't close reliably without systematic tracking. You experience outcomes, but you don't systematically compare them against your original stated expectations. Memory reconstructs your prior confidence in light of what happened. The lesson dissolves into a general sense of having been roughly right or wrong, with no specific signal about where your reasoning process is breaking down. A structured decision log — with confidence scores captured at decision time and reviewed at defined intervals — provides that signal in a form you can actually act on. The tool I built for this is Reflect OS (reflect-os.com). It tracks your calibration automatically as you log decisions, so the curve builds without any manual analysis. But the core mechanism is available to anyone willing to maintain a disciplined decision record.

Something I've come to believe about decision-making that I wish someone had told me earlier: The goal isn't to be right more often. The goal is to know, in advance, how likely you are to be right. These sound similar. They're not. A decision-maker who makes a call at 60% confidence and is wrong has done something right. They correctly identified their uncertainty. They didn't over-resource the contingency, and they didn't under-prepare for failure. Their model of themselves was accurate. A decision-maker who makes a call at 90% confidence and is wrong has a different problem. Not just about that decision — about their reasoning process. Something is generating more certainty than the evidence warrants. This is what confidence calibration measures. Not whether you're right, but whether your confidence predicts your accuracy. Most experienced professionals, when they first see calibration data from their own decisions, find the same thing: they're well-calibrated on routine, frequent decisions, and systematically overconfident on infrequent, high-stakes ones.
