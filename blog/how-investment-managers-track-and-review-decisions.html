How Investment Managers Track and Review Decisions — and Why Most Don't

Investment management is a discipline built on decisions. Yet most investment managers have no systematic process for reviewing those decisions. Here is what the best do differently.

April 2026 · 7 min read

Investment management is, in many ways, an ideal laboratory for studying decision quality. Decisions are discrete, consequential, and — eventually — objectively evaluated by market outcomes. The feedback loop is real, even if it's slow. And yet most investment managers have no systematic process for reviewing their decisions. They have track records, not decision records.

The distinction matters more than it might seem. A track record tells you what happened. A decision record tells you why you expected it to happen, how confident you were, and whether your reasoning held up — or whether you were right for the wrong reasons, or wrong despite good reasoning. These are fundamentally different kinds of information, and only one of them helps you improve.

Investment decisions as hypotheses

The most useful reframe for thinking about investment decision-making is to treat each decision as a hypothesis. You are not just choosing to buy or sell — you are making a testable claim about what will happen, over what time horizon, and with what probability.

A well-formed investment hypothesis has: a specific expected outcome (the position performs in line with X thesis within Y timeframe), a confidence level that reflects genuine uncertainty rather than social presentation, and defined conditions under which the hypothesis would be falsified. This is the same structure as a good scientific hypothesis — and for the same reason. It forces clarity about what you actually believe, and it creates an unambiguous standard against which to evaluate the outcome.

Most investment committee packs contain sophisticated analysis. They contain detailed financial modelling, scenario analysis, risk discussion, and a clear recommendation. What they almost never contain is a numerical confidence level assigned by the decision-maker to the expected outcome, and a specific commitment about what "success" looks like at a defined future date.

Without these two elements, the decision record cannot close the feedback loop. You can look at performance data later and construct a narrative about whether the thesis played out — but the narrative will be contaminated by the outcome. You will remember being more or less confident than you were. You will reinterpret the thesis through the lens of what actually happened.

The problem with post-mortems

Most investment teams do some form of post-mortem analysis on significant decisions — particularly losses. These processes have real value: they surface what went wrong, identify information that wasn't weighted correctly, and produce lessons that inform future decisions.

But post-mortems have a structural problem. They happen after the outcome is known. This means that everything they produce is retrospectively constructed — including the team's recollection of what they believed at the time, how confident they were, and what risks they considered. Research on this is unambiguous: people cannot accurately recall their pre-outcome beliefs after the fact. Memory systematically adjusts toward the outcome through hindsight bias.

The IC pack describes the decision you were prepared to defend. The decision record captures what you actually believed. These are often different documents.

A post-mortem of a decision for which no pre-outcome record exists is essentially a reconstruction exercise. It may produce useful lessons, but it cannot reliably tell you whether the decision was good or bad given what you knew at the time — which is the only question that matters for improving future decisions.

What systematic tracking looks like

The investment managers who have built systematic decision-tracking practices consistently describe the same core elements. Each decision — or at least each position entry and exit — is logged at the time with the expected outcome, a confidence level, and a review date. These logs are distinct from the IC pack. The IC pack is the formal analysis; the decision record is the honest statement of what you actually believe.

Reviews happen at predetermined intervals: typically 90 days after entry to assess whether early indicators are tracking to thesis, and at exit to evaluate the full outcome against the original expectation. The review is structured: what did you expect, what happened, did the thesis hold up, and if not, where did the reasoning fail?

Over time, this produces a database of hypothesis tests. The analysis questions are then: are you well-calibrated across confidence bands — do your 75% confidence calls succeed about 75% of the time? Are there sector or asset class patterns in your calibration? Do your highest-conviction positions outperform your moderate-conviction ones, or are they just higher risk? Is your risk assessment improving or static?

Calibration in investment decision-making

Calibration is particularly important in investment management because the cost of miscalibration is direct and measurable. An overconfident investment manager sizes positions too large relative to their actual accuracy rate. They are surprised more often than they should be. Their portfolio construction is implicitly based on a model of their own accuracy that doesn't match reality.

Conversely, an underconfident manager passes on opportunities they should take, sizes positions too small, and underperforms their actual edge. Both directions of miscalibration are costly, and neither is visible without a systematic record that compares stated confidence to actual outcomes.

The investment managers who track this consistently find that their calibration differs significantly by decision type. Entry decisions and exit decisions may have different calibration profiles. Long and short positions may differ. Decisions made under time pressure from market events may have a different calibration signature than decisions made in a normal IC process.

The IC pack versus the decision record

The investment committee pack is an advocacy document. Its purpose is to present the strongest case for a position in a format that enables the committee to evaluate and approve it. It is optimised for persuasion and completeness of analysis, not for capturing the decision-maker's honest uncertainty.

A decision record is a private document. Its purpose is to create an honest record of what you believed, how confident you were, and what you expected — uncontaminated by the social dynamics of the IC process. It is optimised for learning, not approval.

Both are valuable. They serve different purposes. The best investment managers maintain both — using the IC process for rigour and governance, and a personal decision journal to build the feedback loop that actually improves their judgement over time.
